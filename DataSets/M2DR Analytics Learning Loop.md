
## A Weekly Pedagogical Sequence: **M²DR Loop**

**Method → Meaning → Demonstration → Repurposing**

Many students—especially non-IS majors—struggle in analytics courses not because the methods are too technical, but because they fail to see how those methods connect to the decisions and problems in their own disciplines. The **M²DR Loop (Method → Meaning → Demonstration → Repurposing)** is a weekly instructional sequence designed to solve this problem by making relevance explicit rather than assumed. Each topic begins with the **method**, introduced in discipline-neutral business terms so all students understand what kind of decision the technique supports. The instructor then establishes **meaning** by explaining how that method maps onto different functional contexts (e.g., what “risk,” “performance,” or “segmentation” mean in accounting, management, marketing, or operations). This is followed by a **demonstration** using a concrete dataset to show the method working end-to-end. Finally, students engage in **repurposing**, where they translate the same method to their own major by identifying analogous decisions and datasets in their field. 

In the inaugural pilot of this approach, students also play an active role in **co-creating the course infrastructure** by discovering, proposing, and contributing discipline-specific datasets, which are curated into a shared databank (The Cross Disciplinary Data Bank - CDDB) for future offerings. This design preserves analytical rigor while positioning analytics as a general decision capability—and turns student diversity into a cumulative asset rather than a pedagogical challenge.

## **Inaugural Semester: Guided Translation Mode**

The goal here is *exposure + discipline awareness*, not full independence.

### **Weekly Flow (Instructor-Led → Student-Reflected)**

### 1. Introduce the Method (Universal)

* What the technique does *in business terms* (not math)
* What kinds of decisions it supports
* What it *cannot* tell you

> Example framing:
> “Clustering is not about customers—it’s about **discovering structure when categories are unknown**.”

---

### 2. Introduce a Canonical Dataset (Instructor-Chosen)

* Choose datasets that are:

  * Clean enough to work
  * Intuitively understandable
  * Clearly tied to *one* function (marketing, ops, finance)

This becomes the **anchor example**, not the universal case.

---

### 3. Walk Through the Example (Shared Reality)

* Run the analysis
* Interpret outputs
* Explicitly narrate:

  * “If you were in *another* discipline, this is the part that would change”
  * “This assumption would break in HR / Finance / Healthcare”

This meta-commentary is crucial.

---

### 4. Homework: Structured Translation Exercise

Instead of only “run the model,” add a **discipline-mapping section** to each submission. Require students to do some research using AI and other tools.

**New Required Section (Short but Mandatory):**

> **Discipline Translation**
>
> * My major / intended field:
> * A decision in my field that resembles this problem:
> * What would be the “rows” and “columns” in that context?
> * Is this method currently used in my field?
>
>   * Widely / Rarely / Not at all / Unsure
> * One dataset (real or hypothetical) where this *could* be applied

You’re grading *thinking*, not correctness here.

---

### 5. Dataset Contribution (Low Stakes, High Signal)

Students must either:

* Link to a real dataset **or**
* Describe a dataset that *should* exist (variables + source)

These go into:

> **The IS 4487 Cross-Discipline Dataset Bank (Raw)**

You don’t promise you’ll use them yet—just that they’re contributing.

## **Next Semester: Choice-Based Repurposing Mode**

Now you flip the burden—from *recognition* to *ownership*.

### **Weekly Flow (Method-Stable, Context-Flexible)**

### 1. Introduce the Method (Same as Before)

Minimal changes—this is your stable core.

---

### 2. Demo Dataset (Only as Proof of Life)

* One quick walkthrough
* Purpose: “Here’s what ‘correct’ looks like”

Then you explicitly say:

> “You are not required to use this dataset.”

That sentence matters.

---

### 3. Student-Selected Dataset Application

Students choose:

* A dataset from the shared library **or**
* A new dataset they find / generate

They must:

* Run the *same* analysis
* Keep the method fixed
* Adapt interpretation to their domain

This reinforces **method invariance, context variance**.

---

### 4. Enrichment Reflection (Higher-Order Thinking)

Instead of “what did you get,” ask:

> **Analytical Enrichment Questions**
>
> * What variables are missing that would materially change decisions?
> * What bias or structural limitation exists in this dataset?
> * What stakeholder would disagree with your interpretation—and why?

Now they’re thinking like analysts, not technicians.

---

### 5. Dataset Promotion Pipeline (Instructor Curation)

You quietly upgrade the best submissions into:

* “Verified datasets”
* Tagged by discipline, decision type, and method

Over time, you build:

> A **living, student-sourced analytics corpus**

---

## Why This Works (Pedagogically)

### 1. It Solves the “Why Should I Care?” Problem

Students don’t need to *believe* analytics is relevant—you make them **articulate relevance themselves**.

### 2. It Preserves Rigor

The method never changes. Only the story does.

### 3. It Aligns with Your Philosophy

* CRISP-DM
* Judgment over code
* AI as assistant, not authority
* Analytics as *sensemaking*, not math

### 4. It Future-Proofs the Course

Each cohort:

* Improves your examples
* Expands disciplinary reach
* Reduces prep load over time
